# Don't Cut Philly Out of Pennsylvania's Bipartisan State Zoning Reform Bills

---

Earlier this week we witnessed a major step forward for state-level zoning and housing reform in Pennsylvania, with two bipartisan bills passing out of the House Housing and Community Development committee.

The two bills — which would allow homeowners to build **accessory dwelling units** (small secondary homes on the same lot, sometimes called in-law suites, granny flats, or carriage houses) in residential neighborhoods, and limit municipal powers to cap how many unrelated people can share a single home — represent a sea change in housing politics inside the Capitol.

State lawmakers have grown increasingly comfortable with the idea of state zoning standards bills — legislation that sets minimum zoning requirements every municipality must follow — a necessary precondition for switching Pennsylvania's sluggish rate of home-building onto a faster track. [Pennsylvania ranked 44th nationally in the rate of housing built from 2017 to 2023](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/issue-briefs/2025/03/pennsylvanias-lack-of-building-has-contributed-to-housing-shortage-hurt-affordability), according to a March 2025 Pew brief.

Before the 2023–2024 legislative term, the acceptable scope for state housing policy thinking in Harrisburg was painted into a tight corner. Lawmakers mostly confined themselves to affordable housing funding, blight-fighting ideas, and various tax credits. Everyone\* generally ignored the local zoning and permitting rules — what each town or city decides it will allow built where and how — that determine whether housing is actually built.

Just two years ago, House Republicans on the Local Government Committee opposed thematically-similar bills on a party-line vote, with one House Democrat defecting too. A bipartisan Senate zoning reform bill, SB 1126, attracted early support in 2024 but failed to advance out of committee, and the bipartisan spirit in the Senate didn't carry over to the House.

This session, the two House zoning reform bills passed with unanimous Democratic support and a plurality of the committee's Republican members. Seven committee Republicans still voted no, but five voted yes, and both measures passed 19–7.

## The 'Affordability' Juggernaut and Its Limits

Voters' intense single-minded focus on cost-of-living concerns in 2026 has every elected official at every level of government scrambling for ideas to show voters they're Doing Something to reduce living costs.

There are a lot of new ideas being tossed into the mix, and — maybe more commonly — electeds are engaging in some "now more than ever-ism," remodeling their existing policy priorities with a pro-affordability rhetorical gloss. Any halfway-coherent idea — and many an incoherent one — that purports to reduce the cost of living has more political traction than usual in this moment, and it can be hard to separate the wheat from the chaff.

The PA Housing Choices Coalition and its allies have a whole platform of evidence-based pro-housing policy ideas, drawing on best practices from cities and states like Austin and Seattle — places that have passed broad zoning and land-use reforms and subsequently seen housing permits explode. Many of those ideas are represented in current legislation — a [package of bills from Reps. John Inglis and Tarik Khan and collaborators](CITIZEN ARTICLE LINK).

These reforms would override — in legal terms, "preempt" — some of the main housing-blocking rules on the books at the municipal level: minimum-lot-size requirements, mandatory off-street parking, single-family-only zoning, outright bans on accessory dwellings, and caps on unrelated roommates. They're in competition with other packages of ineffective, voluntary reform bills that borrow the same problem diagnosis and rhetoric about over-regulation, but stop short of doing anything with real teeth. Many state politicians want to be seen as on the side of affordability, but they're still politicians, and they still don't generally enjoy grappling with hard trade-offs or making choices that upset some voters and interest groups.

Housing reform is popular, according to recent polling from YouGov, prepared for the PA Housing Choices Coalition — a coalition of over 50 organizations from across the ideological spectrum supporting pro-housing legislation in Harrisburg.

What makes housing reform so politically devilish, however, is that while the benefits of building more housing are broad-based, they're also diffuse — while the downsides are more concentrated. It's no fun living next door to a construction site, and people are worried about traffic and parking and all the other very localized annoyances that come with more home-building activity and population density.

Nearly every piece of the current planning system in Pennsylvania — from the state, to the counties, to municipal government — is set up to over-weight the concerns of hyper-local aggrieved parties relative to the broad public interest in widely-attainable and abundant housing in the parts of Pennsylvania where demand is highest and prices are rising fastest, like the Southeast.

That's one reason why Pennsylvania ranks 44th out of 50 states in the share of new housing stock constructed between 2019 and 2023 — there's excessive "stop" power written into every layer of the system, with vanishingly few sources of "go" power in support of more building. One way of understanding the state zoning reform agenda is that it's about reversing this dynamic and removing sources of "stop" power from the various places they live.

The builders' own data makes the cost of this regulatory drag concrete. A [2024 study by Home Innovation Research Labs](https://housingalliancepa.org/wp-content/uploads/Government-regulation-PA-Builder-report-2024_FINAL-1.pdf), commissioned by the Pennsylvania Builders Association, found that government regulation accounts for **29.5% of the price of the average new single-family home in Pennsylvania — $212,700 on a $721,600 home, and noticeably higher than the 23.8% national average**. In the five-county Philadelphia region, regulation eats up 29.2% of the price of a new home, and the region reported the highest regulatory burden in the state — as a share of a new home's price — for building code changes over the past decade and for municipal architectural design mandates. From the moment a builder files a zoning application, it takes an average of 23.2 months before site work can even start. And for every $1,000 added to a new home's price, another **2,640 Pennsylvania households are priced out of the market**.

The trend is accelerating. That same study found construction code changes added 4.5% to a new home's cost between 2014 and 2019, and then nearly doubled to 9.1% between 2019 and 2024. And the places Senate leaders are reportedly trying to carve out of the reform bills — Philadelphia and Pittsburgh — are the two metro areas where the regulatory share of a new home's selling price is highest in the state.

## Why Philly Zoning Politics Needs a State Rescue

This is particularly important for Philadelphia, where — because of **Councilmanic Prerogative**, the unwritten but ironclad rule that the Council district member gets the final word on any land-use decision within their district — it's become almost impossible in practice to pass citywide housing laws anymore.

The way Council approaches **zoning overlay districts** in Philly — the neighborhood-specific zoning layers that sit on top of the citywide base code and change the rules for that particular area — is not normal relative to the rest of the state. The usual idea with an overlay is to create **density bonuses** (rules that let builders put up more housing if they meet some public-interest condition, like including affordable units) or other additive incentives to achieve a policy goal. In Philadelphia, overlays are most often used to *nullify* broad-based changes in certain areas.

Since 2019 — when then-Councilmember María Quiñones-Sánchez passed the Mixed-Income Housing density bonus, [the last major citywide housing law Council has enacted](https://thephiladelphiacitizen.org/does-council-want-to-limit-housing/) — overlay districts have mostly been deployed in the opposite direction, to peel Council districts out of anything citywide their members don't like. The pattern scaled up sharply with Bill No. 210078-A in April 2021, which simultaneously created new district overlays in the 4th and 8th, banned ADUs across the 4th, 5th, 8th, and 9th districts' RSA-5 rowhouse zones, and entrenched the dimensional fragmentation described below.

As one example of the type of dysfunction this governing dynamic produces, the section of the code for rowhouses — Philly's most ubiquitous housing type — now contains **at least 13 different sets of dimensional rules** for how to build one, depending on which Council district or neighborhood a lot happens to sit in. One base code, plus a dozen district- and neighborhood-specific overlays that modify height, setbacks, lot coverage, or minimum lot area.

Accessory dwellings, which the House committee voted to legalize statewide this week, are another good example of the problem. They've been **banned outright in four City Council districts since 2021, and across most of the Northeast via two ward-based overlays**. The more suburban-style neighborhoods where ADUs make the most sense are mostly off-limits, and there's no practical local path to changing that. If a state bill makes it to Governor Shapiro's desk and he signs it, accessory dwellings will be broadly legal **by-right** — meaning an owner can build one without needing special permission from the Zoning Board — all across Philadelphia, where lot dimensions allow.

The only realistic way we can get back to passing citywide housing laws again is for the state government to step in and directly legalize certain housing and building types by-right — putting the blocking power outside of City Council's reach more often. The Councilmanic Prerogative toolbox will still have plenty of tools in it even if state-level reformers somehow win this round of Whack-a-Mole, and the idea that state reform could end Prerogative is seriously overblown. But it's also the most workable idea anybody's come up with for how to disrupt these dynamics in practice.

Other bills badly needed in Philly and now under consideration at the state level, or slated to be, include a **transit-oriented development (TOD)** law making it easier to build housing near train, bus, and trolley stations; a law allowing mixed-use and multifamily housing on commercially-zoned sites like malls, office parks, and downtowns; and laws reforming off-street parking minimums (which force builders to provide a set number of spots whether the future residents want them or not) and use regulations.

Unfortunately, while the Inglis bill package intends\*\* to include Philadelphia and Pittsburgh — the two **Home Rule cities** (Pennsylvania cities that write their own charters and are governed by locally-passed laws rather than the standard state municipal code) that aren't regulated by PA's Municipalities Planning Code like everywhere else — the Senate looks to be headed in a different direction. Both major Senate zoning reform bills would leave Philadelphia out.

A bipartisan group including Senate Republicans Greg Rothman (the PA GOP party chair) and Dan Laughlin, and Democrats Nikil Saval and Tim Kearney, has been working on its own package of zoning and housing reform bills similar in spirit to the Inglis package. So far, they've released their own version of an accessory dwellings bill — broadly compatible with Inglis's — with more bills supposedly on the drawing board.

Another bipartisan group — reportedly including Republican Senators Jarrett Coleman, Elder Vogel, and Dawn Keefer and Democrat Nikil Saval (**TK CHECK with Coalition staff**) — would allow residential buildings in commercially-zoned areas.

The Rothman/Saval bill currently includes Philadelphia and Pittsburgh, but according to sources, Rothman has signaled he intends to remove them at the request of others. The Coleman bill as-written includes Pittsburgh but leaves out Philadelphia.

It's still unclear exactly what is happening or why, but sources closer to the talks say some combination of Senate Republican leadership, Urban Affairs and Housing Committee staff, and Senator Joe Picozzi of Northeast Philadelphia has requested that one or more of the two biggest cities be removed from the legislation. To accomplish this, the bills would amend the **Municipalities Planning Code** — the state law that governs zoning and planning for most Pennsylvania towns but *not* Philadelphia and Pittsburgh — instead of **Title 53** of the Consolidated Statutes, which applies more broadly. That choice of statute is what determines whether Philly and Pittsburgh are in or out.

For all the reasons above about planning dysfunction in Philadelphia, this would be a serious mistake. And from a fairness standpoint, letting the two biggest cities off the hook for changes everyone else would have to make is a sure-fire way to poison the politics of the whole project. The entire essence of the proposition reformers are offering is that *a little bit more housing everywhere* solves the problem. Once you create the precedent of exempting the heavy-hitters, other towns will start asking for exemptions too.

Returning to the point about evidence-based policy, a bill that excludes the two biggest cities simply won't come close to resolving Pennsylvania's housing shortage. According to Governor Shapiro's own [Housing Action Plan 2026–2035](https://dced.pa.gov/download/housing-action-plan-2026/?wpdmdl=128767), the Commonwealth needs to build **450,000 new homes by 2035**, and the five-county Southeast region alone accounts for **206,839 of those units — roughly 46% of the statewide need, and more than double any other region's**. Widen the lens to the full I-95/I-78/I-81 growth corridor, and it's closer to 57%. Any reform bill serious about the state's housing shortage has to include the place with the largest housing demand; excluding Philadelphia isn't a compromise, it's a concession that the bill won't meet its own goals.

The Shapiro administration's plan also specifically calls for both of the reforms the House just passed. It recommends that the Commonwealth "limit restrictive occupancy limits targeted at unrelated individuals" (the Golden Girls Law) and expand "Elder Cottage Housing Opportunity programs" (accessory dwellings). Leaving the state's largest city out of policies the administration itself endorses would be a strange way to follow the governor's lead.

While it's true that Philadelphia's rate of housing construction has vastly outpaced our suburban neighbors' since **YEAR TK**, and that some catch-up growth outside the city is long overdue, the idea that we can meaningfully shift Southeast PA's housing growth track while cutting the entire city of Philadelphia out of any permitting-reform deal is foolhardy.

This only becomes a live issue if the bills all pass their respective chambers, which would set up the legislative coalitions for a showdown in conference committee where the bills' divergent details would have to be reconciled into a single final package.

Here's what's odd about this effort to carve out Philadelphia: **Philadelphia's own House delegation is firmly on the record in support**. Six of the 14 Democrats who voted Yea on the committee represent Philadelphia districts — Morgan Cephas, G. Roni Green, Amen Brown, Rick Krajewski, Ismail Smith-Wade-El, and Ben Waxman. Another half-dozen Philly-area House Democrats cosponsored one or both bills, including Tarik Khan (the lead sponsor on the Golden Girls bill), Malcolm Kenyatta, Keith Harris, Benjamin Sanchez, and Jared Solomon.

The picture becomes even sharper when you stack the House vote against the Senate map. In Senate District 7 (West and Southwest Philadelphia), represented by Senator Anthony Hardy Williams, **every House member nested within his district supported one or both bills** — three Yea votes on the committee plus the prime sponsor of the Golden Girls bill. In the Senate districts of Vincent Hughes, Sharif Street, Art Haywood, Christine Tartaglione, and Nikil Saval, the pattern holds: at least one, often several, of the House members their constituents send to Harrisburg are cosponsors or committee Yea votes.

You cannot find a single Democratic Philadelphia Senate district whose nested House delegation opposes these bills.

The exception is Senator Joe Picozzi's Senate District 5 in Northeast Philadelphia — where, tellingly, Picozzi's Democratic predecessor Jimmy Dillon was a cosponsor of SB 1126, the Senate's 2023–24 zoning omnibus. Dillon's departure flipped one of Philadelphia's Senate voices from pro-reform to the person reportedly now working hardest to exempt the city.
