## New paragraph: regulatory burden (drop into "Affordability Juggernaut" section)

*Place this right after the paragraph that begins "What makes housing reform so politically devilish…" and before "Nearly every piece of the current planning system…"*

> The builders' own data makes the cost of this regulatory drag concrete. A 2024 study by [Home Innovation Research Labs](https://housingalliancepa.org/wp-content/uploads/Government-regulation-PA-Builder-report-2024_FINAL-1.pdf), commissioned by the Pennsylvania Builders Association, found that government regulation accounts for **29.5% of the price of the average new single-family home in Pennsylvania — $212,700 on a $721,600 home, and noticeably higher than the 23.8% national average**. In the five-county Philadelphia region, regulation eats up **29.2%** of the price of a new home, and the region reported the highest regulatory cost in the state — as a share of a new home's price — for building code changes over the past decade and architectural design mandates. From the moment a builder files a zoning application, it takes an average of **23.2 months** before they can start site work. For every additional **$1,000** tacked onto a new home's price, another **2,640 Pennsylvania households** get priced out of the market.
>
> And the trend is accelerating: the same study found construction code changes added 4.5% to a new home's cost between 2014 and 2019 — and then nearly doubled, to 9.1%, between 2019 and 2024. The cities that some state senators are reportedly trying to carve out of reform bills — Philadelphia and Pittsburgh — are the two places where the regulatory share of a new home's selling price is highest in the state.

## Replacement paragraph: Shapiro-plan / regional need

*Replace the paragraph that currently reads "Returning to the point about evidence-based policy, a bill that excludes the two biggest cities..."*

> Returning to the point about evidence-based policy, a bill that excludes Philadelphia and Pittsburgh is not going to come close to addressing the scale of Pennsylvania's housing shortage. According to Governor Shapiro's own *[Housing Action Plan 2026–2035](https://dced.pa.gov/download/housing-action-plan-2026/?wpdmdl=128767)*, the Commonwealth needs to build **450,000 new homes by 2035**, and the five-county Southeast region alone accounts for **206,839** of those units — roughly **46% of the statewide need, and more than double any other region's**. Widen the lens to the full I-95/I-78/I-81 growth corridor, and it's closer to 57%. Any reform bill serious about the state's housing shortage has to include the place with the largest housing demand; excluding Philadelphia is not a compromise, it's a concession that the bill won't meet its own goals.
>
> The Shapiro administration's plan also specifically calls for both of the reforms the House just passed. The plan recommends that the Commonwealth *"limit restrictive occupancy limits targeted at unrelated individuals"* (the Golden Girls Law) and expand *"Elder Cottage Housing Opportunity programs"* (accessory dwellings). Leaving the state's largest city out of policies the administration itself endorses would be a strange way to follow the governor's lead.
