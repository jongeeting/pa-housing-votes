Housing Bill Prioritization for the 2027-28 Session

With unified Democratic control of Harrisburg, the floodgates are about to open: many housing bills will move in 2027-28. This memo shows decision makers which housing supply reforms already have public support, legislator support or the Governor's backing. It also gives each concept an overall score, which rates its effectiveness, low administrative burden, low fiscal cost, fit with the Governor's priorities and legislative momentum. It then recommends where to start. Other groups will push their own bills too. This memo lays out the record behind ours.

Bottom line

  • Lead with ADUs, then occupancy. ADUs have the highest overall score (56.7 of 60): a perfect policy score and the strongest legislative momentum of any zoning concept. Use HB 2186 as the starting point, review the Senate bills (SB 1346, SB 1256 (ADUs)) for improvements, and produce one ADU bill that covers every municipality, including Philadelphia and Pittsburgh. Occupancy (SB 1263) is next: the House passed its companion 123-78.
  • Strong policies still need momentum. Commercial-zone housing, parking mandates, missing middle, minimum lot size (starter homes on smaller lots) and manufactured homes score 45-47 of 60: near-perfect policy, little legislative record yet. They need bills and champions.
  • Move one concept per bill. SB 1126 (2023-24 zoning omnibus), backed by Sens. Rothman, Laughlin, Saval and Kearney, supplies text for several standalone bills.
  • Top new bill: housing on faith-owned and state-owned land. The Governor's plan names it and no one has introduced it.
  • Fill the Senate gaps. Parking mandates, minimum lot size and manufactured homes have no Senate bill. Lot size and manufactured homes have no bill in either chamber.
  • Preservation is a separate track. Tangled title, home repair and the historic tax credit have broad support but add few homes, and the last two need state money. They complement supply; they can't substitute for it.

1. The landscape

  • The House was never the problem. HB 2186 (ADUs) passed 139-62 and HB 2109 (occupancy) 123-78. Both died in the Republican-controlled Senate Urban Affairs committee. Under unified control, these bills can get a Senate vote.
  • What passed got weaker to pass. SB 1281 (pre-approved housing plans) applied only narrowly, and even then passed the Senate 50-0 only after an amendment required projects to comply with existing zoning. In the House, HB 2109 (occupancy) picked up an explicit five-person cap on unrelated housemates before it passed. The 2027-28 versions should return to the stronger texts.
  • The Governor's agenda points to statewide standards. His budget address calls for a catalog of local zoning rules, and his Housing Action Plan proposes model ordinances and a "Housing-Ready" designation. Statewide standards deliver the same goals without asking DCED to review 2,560 municipalities one by one. He named "standards for accessory dwelling units," transit-oriented development, and mixed use on commercial corridors, and the plan names lot size, setbacks, manufactured homes, parking and occupancy (pp. 26, 32). The plan is silent on preemption; nothing in it argues against a statewide standard.
  • Coverage matters. Bills that amend the Municipalities Planning Code skip Philadelphia and Pittsburgh. Each bill should include Philadelphia and Pittsburgh.

Funding: a housing bond

The Governor proposed "a new $1 billion Critical Infrastructure Fund, supported by the issuing of bonds, that will go toward housing" (budget address). His plan says the fund should use "a combination of grants, low-interest loans, and loan guarantees" and support "middle market housing development" (p. 20). Borrowing costs have risen since, which makes any bond more expensive. A bond is still the most direct way to pay for the parts of the agenda that need money. BPN would put the proceeds toward two uses:

  • A revolving loan fund for mixed-income housing, modeled on Montgomery County, Maryland's $100 million Housing Production Fund. It makes five-year construction loans to mixed-income projects with at least 30% of units income-restricted, then relends the money as it is repaid (HOC, NACo).
  • Home repair (HB 1650/SB 876), which has gone three budgets without state money (Capital-Star).

The bond and the zoning bills reinforce each other: by-right rules lower what each project costs, and the fund pays for more of them.

Who enforces? An open design question

A statewide standard is only as strong as its enforcement. Today the main enforcer is the applicant who gets denied: validity challenges, curative amendments and deemed-approval suits are slow, paid for by the applicant, and award no attorney's fees. Enforcement should be settled alongside the standards themselves. The specific choices lawmakers make will determine how much it asks of the state, so we do not score it yet.

Options under discussion, which could be combined:

  • County planning commissions flagging noncompliance in the zoning reviews they already conduct.
  • DCED review of municipalities, ideally triggered by complaints rather than routine audits.
  • Attorney General authority to sue noncompliant municipalities. Pennsylvania has a precedent in the ACRE law for local agricultural ordinances (Act 38 of 2005).
  • A private right of action for applicants, residents, housing advocacy organizations and public-interest law firms, with attorney's fees.
  • Loss of discretionary state grant eligibility for noncompliant municipalities, including high-usage programs such as DCNR parkland grants.

One principle holds regardless of the details: complaint-driven enforcement asks far less of the state than audit-driven verification.

7. Preservation track: separate from supply

Complements supply reform; doesn't replace it

These bills keep existing homes and buildings livable, owned and in use. They have some of the broadest support in the legislature, but they add few or no new homes. Home repair and a larger historic credit also need state money, while most of the supply agenda costs the state nothing. Passing these instead of the supply bills would leave the shortage where it is.

  • Tangled title. The plan counts about 35,000 heirs' properties statewide and over 10,000 tangled titles in Philadelphia, and names "transfer-on-death deeds for primary residences" (pp. 15, 24). HB 2124 (transfer-on-death deeds) passed the House 202-0. SB 890 (Bartolotta, R) cleared Senate Judiciary 14-0. No state cost beyond court and county process.
  • Home repair. HB 1650 (Home Preservation Grant Program) passed the House 122-80. It would succeed the 2022 Whole-Home Repairs program, which passed with bipartisan support and $125 million in one-time federal aid; 3,965 of about 17,000 applicants got help (Capital-Star). The plan quotes roundtable participants calling Whole-Home Repairs "key to maintaining our naturally occurring affordable housing" and counts 16,500 units on repair waitlists (pp. 11-12). Republicans have since pulled their support. In 2024 the money stalled after the Governor signed the budget, with Senate Republicans saying it needed enabling legislation (WHYY). The 2025-26 budget left it out, the third year without a state-funded successor (Capital-Star). House Republicans voted 20-80 on HB 1650. The Senate version, SB 876, is co-authored by Sens. Argall (R) and Saval (D). The plan calls for a "state-sponsored home preservation fund" (p. 21). It needs an annual appropriation.
  • Historic preservation tax credit. The credit pays for rehabilitating historic buildings, including conversions to housing. Current law caps it at $20 million a year statewide and $500,000 per taxpayer. HB 1572 (Goughnour, D) passed the House 141-62 (D 102-0, R 39-62). It and the Senate bills, SB 1356 (Flynn, D) and SB 1394 (Farry, R), would raise only the per-taxpayer cap, to $1.5 million (bill text). None raises the $20 million program cap. Peer states go much further: Kentucky's cap rose from $5 million to $100 million a year (Novogradac), and Ohio's base cap was $60 million, temporarily doubled to $120 million for 2023-24 (Vorys, 2022). BPN's version would remove the per-project cap or raise it sharply and lift the program cap toward those levels. It is a tax expenditure, so it scores 1 of 5 on fiscal cost. The plan supports state money for conversions and upper-floor rehab (p. 20) but does not name the credit.

8. Recommended changes to bill text

  1. Every bill: introduce it in both chambers with the same initial text, to minimize the differences that must be reconciled after each chamber acts.
  2. Every by-right bill: add SB 1256's clause making noncompliant local ordinances void by a date, with the state standard applying meanwhile.
  3. ADUs: use HB 2186 as the starting point, clarify and strengthen its minimum building and impervious coverage entitlement, and add a 14-day deemed approval.
  4. Occupancy: use SB 1263's clean ban; drop HB 2109's five-person authorization and student carve-out.
  5. Missing middle: narrow HB 2185's proof-of-concurrency clause.
  6. Commercial zones: drop the growing-county test in HB 1459 (housing in commercial zones) and add the density, lot-size and setback waivers from SB 1239.
  7. Grants: replace application-based grants (SB 1278/HB 2423) with a formula payment per new permitted unit per capita, from Census Building Permits Survey data.
  8. Small-estate affidavit: make SB 1203/HB 2172 (small-estate home affidavit) mandatory statewide, not a county opt-in.
  9. Single-stair: turn SR 348 (single-stair study) from a study into a statutory directive with a deadline.

9. Recommendations

  1. Pass a single statewide ADU bill early in the session, combining the House-passed HB 2186 with the Senate's 14-day deemed-approval process.
  2. Move occupancy reform (SB 1263) early.
  3. Introduce standalone Senate bills on parking mandates, minimum lot size reform and manufactured homes, building on the SB 1126 coalition, and House bills on lot size and manufactured homes.
  4. Introduce a bill for housing on faith-owned and state-owned land, a named action in the Governor's plan and BPN's top priority among new bills, and consider adding land owned by other governmental entities such as counties, school districts and transit agencies. Pair it with faster land bank disposition.
  5. Make local permitting faster, more certain and accountable. Staff approval against clear standards, an optional third-party review track on a deadline, and fee refunds when towns lose. The Governor's plan calls for "speed, certainty, and efficiency" in local approvals (p. 31), and 79% of voters, including 78% of Republicans, back permitting reform. No bill has been introduced.
  6. Advance parking mandate reform (HB 2155), commercial-zone housing (SB 1239, covering all municipalities) and missing middle (HB 2185) as standalone bills.
  7. Settle enforcement alongside the standards, favoring complaint-driven review over state audits of 2,560 municipalities.
  8. Introduce a transit-oriented development bill to match the Governor's call in the budget address.
  9. Back the plan's financing tools alongside the zoning bills: the Residential Reinvestment Tax Credit, a higher PHARE cap, funds for small builders and the state housing credit for small projects.
  10. Require regular property reassessment (SB 567 and the Mazzocco-Kinkead House memo), so new homes stop paying higher effective tax rates than older ones.
  11. Pursue a housing bond for a mixed-income revolving loan fund (the Montgomery County, Maryland model) and home repair, building on the Governor's $1 billion bond-backed fund.
  12. Legalize single-stair apartment buildings up to six stories by statute, building on the 14 senators who signed SR 348, rather than waiting on a study.
  13. On the separate preservation track, pass tangled-title reform (SB 890), fund home repair (HB 1650/SB 876) and expand the historic tax credit's program cap, not just its per-project cap, alongside, not instead of, the supply bills.
  14. Release the Municipalities Planning Code study (SR 211). Its scope covers single-family zoning, lot sizes, setbacks and missing middle, and it was due in October 2026.

Method and caveats

  • Data: bill text, sponsors, cosponsors, histories and roll calls from palegis.us (fetched Nov 5, 2026); member-level votes from the PA Housing Votes tracker plus palegis roll calls; Governor statements from the budget address and Housing Action Plan (printed page numbers).
  • Scoring: effectiveness rewards self-executing statewide standards; admin burden and fiscal cost are inverted (5 = no agency capacity needed; 5 = no state cost), and a bill scoring 0-1 on admin cannot rank above Tier 3. Fiscal cost: 4 = administrative cost only or opt-in local revenue, 3 = redirects existing state money, 2 = new sizable spending, 1 = large recurring appropriation or tax expenditure. Lawmaker support is computed, not judged. Concepts and bills both get one overall score out of 60: 45 for the policy and 15 for legislative momentum. Supply and preservation are scored the same way but ranked separately. We do not score housing units unlocked, because no estimates exist.
  • Caveats: November 2026 results were unofficial at the time of writing; counts exclude members not returning in 2027, and new members have no record yet. Preservation bills score low on effectiveness by design.
